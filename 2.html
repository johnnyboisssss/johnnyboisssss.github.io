Extrospection Is Incredibly Important

Oct 17, 2026

In my life, I have spent a lot of time thinking. One of my most powerful defense mechanisms has been turning inward, asking myself questions, and ultimately deflecting the insecurity I was feeling away through rationalizations. Stoicism was a huge passion of mine because it gave me license to repress my emotions by appealing to apatheia. Passions, as they're called by Stoics, are only felt by those who cannot properly distinguish what is in their control and what isn't. I'd say to myself, "Only what you can control--your will--can truly harm your soul. The body is meaningless compared to reason." But things that aren't in your control can still hurt you. The solution is not to stop valuing and become an sagely statue. This only guarantees that it will burst out at some later point. Feeling that pain--expressing it, that is how you come back to reality. You need to allow yourself to heal rather than holding the wound open trying to convince yourself it never existed in the first place. The solution is to feel the pain fully. That acknowledgment and acceptance of your body and your emotions is one of the most important psychological steps in the life journey of a rationalist. I fear what would have happened had I continued in my rationalistic ways, constantly fighting to understand a world that simply wouldn't fit into my rigid theories, constantly repressing my insecurities, becoming ever convinced that the only way forward was finding the key: the perfect rationalist theory to annihiliate all others. I've always wanted to understand myself, but rationalism--pure introspection, it has never worked for me. What does work?

There is no extrospection without introspection. You must extrospect in order to introspect. The Dzogchen Buddhists understand this well. When you look within for a self, you don't find anything. When your mind is totally focused on introspecting, it comes up blank. What's funny is that when you do this meditation, you inevitably end up paying attention to the world around you, usually in sharper detail. You can only understand yourself by reference to reality--to what is out there. I notice this when I talk to girls I'm nervous around. I used to look within attempting to find the right things to say. I'd blank out and mumble out some nonsense. It was really bad when I was insecure because I was so obssessed with "thinking before I spoke," AKA introspecting before I spoke, that I rarely ever had anything to say to women or men except for contrived open-ended questions. If I asked them something broad and deep enough, I could usually get away with my out-of-control interoception. Women never really appreciated this. I could really only talk to men because they were more interested in ideas. They possessed an openness to listen longer and see the virtues I did possess. This was how I made and maintained the few friends I had.

Exteroception is what I was missing. I started doing it more naturally as I became more secure, but applying it purposefully made all the difference. I first learned of it through talking to my friend Abraham. He taught me about a concept called flow. I had heard of flow years before, but he applied it concretely in a way I found very useful. He told me a story about his friends: when they were younger, they'd get together in his garage with some instruments to play music. He said, “We weren’t very good and didn’t know any chords, but when we hit a groove we all felt it and tried to stay in it. You can’t plan a groove; it just spontaneously happens. Then over time you begin to be able to find it faster and faster." He told me that if I tried to be rational and calculating, I'd fail because the information comes too quickly to process consciously. This is true, but there are also more intellectual streams of information that can only be processed in a fast, automatized way. I think that, fundamentally, when you're being a rationalist--a chronic interoceiver, you are turning yourself away from picking up on what you need: reality. When you talk to a girl, you should be looking at her, feeling her, getting into a flow with her. You need to be extrospecting. In this case, you need to focus on her mouth moving, her eyes on yours, etc. This idea is very generlizable. You should be searching for all your values by extrospection. When I'm sitting at home feeling bored and lonely, asking myself, "what do I want in life?" will usually only make me feel sad and behind. I will dream up these vague, abstract ideals to fill the void my question created. "I want a good family." "I want to be a good person." "I want to be wealthy and independent." I might perceive a few random, distant affordances, but this sort of questioning never tells me what I need to do now. It doesn't tell me how to act, it simply reminds me of my lack of those things. It's almost demotivating because this sort of rationalistic introspection gives me no guide on how to achieve my goals. So should you avoid introspection?

When is introspection warranted? How can one know to do it? The answer is the same as to how introspection is possible at all. You should use the evidence provided by reality. In other words, you should determine when to introspect by extrospecting. For example, when a girl asks you to think really hard about a question, and it turns out to be some silly flirtation, ask her if she knows what a dork she is. Look into her eyes, listen to her laugh, feel her, and you'll know what to say and do. Your focus is turned on to the information you need to make a decision. When she asks you a deeper question, you can think for a moment, while switching back often to check on her. You'll be prompted to introspect by reality. There is no way of contriving what you should be thinking. You can only know by extrospection what it is you need to introspect about. With emotions, the story is the same. You feel the pain in your chest and the lump in your throat in response to something you perceive. Sadness is not when you calculate that you lack meaningful values by checking your present circumstances against a list of values stored somewhere in your mind.

Rationalistic, chronic introspection is the result of retreating from reality. It is introspecting without reason or material. It is arbitrarily turning away from the friend you're with because you're worried that you won't be able to determine the right thing to respond. Healthy introspection is justified by one's extrospection. By purposefully looking at the person in front of them, or the places they can go, or the things they can do, it becomes easy to feel pulled in a direction, but is that the right direction?

When you look at reality, you feel pulled emotionally toward certain things. You may feel as though those are values. It is perhaps the most important thing in this article to say that you should follow those emotions, but while validating them through induction. By induction, I mean checking that this value you feel pulled toward harmonizes with all the other values you are pursuing. For instance, if you want to improve your sex drive, you should ensure that you are eating the foods that will achieve that goal. If you look at a girl and want to start a family with her, and that would require mutual loyalty, sleeping with another girl would contradict that. Your values are knowledge about reality--they're knowledge about what you should do. Reality is consistent. A is A. Your knowledge is of reality, so it is also non-contradictory. Ethical knowledge is identically harmonious. The concordance of one's life is the defining mark of its beauty.
