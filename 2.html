Extrospection Is Incredibly Important

Oct 17, 2026

In my life, I've spent a lot of time thinking. My primary defense mechanism consisted of asking myself questions to ultimately deflect the insecurity I was feeling away through rationalizations. Stoicism was once a great passion of mine because it gave me license to repress my emotions by appealing to apatheia. Passions, as they're called by Stoics, are only felt by those who cannot properly distinguish what is in their control and what isn't. I'd say to myself, "Only what you can control--your will--can truly harm your soul. The body is meaningless compared to reason," but things that aren't in your control can still hurt you. The solution is not to stop valuing and become a sagely statue. Rather, feeling that pain--expressing it--is how you come back to reality. You need to allow yourself to heal rather than holding the wound open trying to convince yourself it never existed in the first place. The acknowledgment and acceptance of your body and your emotions is one of the most important psychological steps in the life journey of a rationalist. I fear what would have happened had I continued in my rationalistic ways, constantly fighting to understand a world that refused to fit into my floating theories, constantly repressing my insecurities, and becoming ever convinced that the only way forward was finding the key: the perfect rationalist theory to annihilate all others. I've always wanted to understand myself, but rationalism--pure introspection, it has never worked for me. What does work?

There is no extrospection without introspection. The Dzogchen Buddhists understand this well. When you're focused on introspecting, it comes up blank. Funnily enough, when you do this meditation, you inevitably end up paying attention to the world around you, usually in sharper detail. You can only understand yourself by reference to the out there that you inhabit. I noticed this when I'd talk to girls I was nervous around. I used to look within attempting to find the right things to say. I'd always blank out and mumble some nonsense. It was really bad when I was insecure because I was so obssessed with "thinking before I spoke," AKA introspecting before I spoke, that I rarely ever had anything to say to women or men besides contrived open-ended questions. If I asked them something broad and deep enough, I could latch onto something I knew about, allowing me to get away with my out-of-control interoception. Women never appreciated this. I mostly only talked to men because they were more interested in ideas. They possessed an openness to listen longer and got the chance to see the virtues I did possess. This was how I made and maintained the few friends I had.

Exteroception is what I was missing. I started doing it more naturally as I became more secure, but applying it purposefully made all the difference. I first learned of it talking to my friend Abraham. He taught me about flow. He told me a story about his friends: when they were younger, they'd get together in his garage with some instruments to play music. He said, “We weren’t very good and didn’t know any chords, but when we hit a groove we all felt it and tried to stay in it. You can’t plan a groove; it just spontaneously happens. Then over time you begin to be able to find it faster and faster." He told me that if I tried to be rational and calculating, I'd fail because the information comes too quickly to process consciously. Fundamentally, when you're being a rationalist--a chronic interoceiver, you are turning yourself away from picking up on what you need: reality. When you talk to a girl, you should focus on her mouth moving, her eyes on yours, the way your body reacts to hers, etc. You need to be extrospecting. This idea is very generalizable. You should be searching for all your values by extrospection. When I'm sitting at home feeling bored and lonely, asking myself, "what do I want in life?" will usually only make me feel sad and behind. I will dream up these vague, abstract ideals to fill the void my question created. "I want a good family." "I want to be a good person." "I want to be wealthy and independent." I might recall a few vague, distant affordances, but this sort of questioning never tells me what I need to do now. It doesn't inform my action, it simply reminds me of my lack of those vague values. It's a negative loop: I feel down, I ask what I want, I fail to dream up a way of fixing it, I feel down. So should you avoid introspection?

When is introspection warranted? How can one know to do it? The answer is the same as to how introspection is possible at all. You should use the evidence provided by reality. In other words, you should determine when to introspect by extrospecting. For example, when a girl asks you to think really hard about a question, and it turns out to be some silly flirtation, ask her if she knows what a dork she is. Look into her eyes, listen to her laugh, feel her, and you'll know what to say and do. Your focus is turned on to the information you need to make a decision. When she asks you a deeper question, you can think for a moment, while switching back often to check on her. You'll be prompted to introspect by reality. There is no way of contriving what you should be thinking. You can only know by extrospection what it is you need to introspect about. With emotions, the story is the same. You feel the pain in your chest and the lump in your throat in response to something you perceive. Sadness is not when you calculate that you lack meaningful values by checking your present circumstances against a list of values stored somewhere in your mind.

Rationalistic, chronic introspection is the result of retreating from reality. It is introspecting without reason or material. It's turning your attention inward and away from a person you're with because you're worried you'll fail to find a witty response. Healthy introspection is justified by one's extrospection. By purposefully looking at the person in front of you, or the places you can go, or the things you can do, it becomes easy to feel pulled in a direction, but is that the right direction?

When you look at reality, you feel pulled emotionally toward certain things. You may feel as though those are values. It is perhaps the most important thing in this article to say that you should follow those emotions, but while validating them inductively. By induction, I mean checking that this value you feel pulled toward harmonizes with all the other values you are pursuing. For instance, if you want to improve your sex drive, you should ensure that you are eating the foods that will achieve that goal. If you look at a girl and want to start a family with her, and that would require mutual loyalty, sleeping with another girl would contradict that. Your values are knowledge about reality--they're knowledge about what you should do. Reality is consistent. A is A. Your knowledge is of reality, so your values must be non-contradictory. The concordance of one's life is the defining mark of its beauty. Get out there and extrospect.
